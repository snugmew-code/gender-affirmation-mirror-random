You stand before the bathroom mirror with your arms folded, wondering who allowed such a disgraceful little room to exist.

The sink is cluttered. The towel hangs crooked. A ridiculous poster watches from the wall. None of it belongs beside a woman of your rank.

Your rank.

The thought settles comfortably, like the weight of your officer’s cap. Its stiff brim casts a sharp shadow across your face, framing one cool, watchful eye and a smile so slight it might be mistaken for politeness.

It would be a mistake.

Your long silver hair falls straight down your back, smooth and unadorned. When you turn your head, the fine strands whisper against your high collar. No curls, no ribbons, no nonsense. You appreciate the simplicity. Anything that cannot survive a campaign has no place on your person.

Your uniform fits exquisitely. Heavy black wool embraces your waist, brass buttons gleam across your chest, and your medals rest proudly above your heart. Below your broad leather belt, the long, tailored tunic accentuates your hips before falling over fitted black riding breeches. Your legs feel wonderfully strong beneath them, ending in tall, polished leather riding boots. Their rigid shafts embrace your calves, their modest heels clicking authoritatively against the bathroom tiles.

Leather creaks when you straighten, and the sheathed sabre at your hip nudges your thigh with the reassuring familiarity of an old friend.

You adjust your officer's cap, admiring the cold elegance of your reflection. The delicate golden monocle over your right eye catches the light, its slender chain resting against your cheek.

Then your fingers approach your eyepatch.

Ah.

That.

The black leather presses snugly against your ruined left eye socket. Beneath it, uneven scar tissue pulls uncomfortably whenever your expression changes. The skin feels strangely tight, some patches permanently numb, others almost painfully sensitive. Deep inside, where your eye should be, an occasional phantom ache reminds you of something you can no longer possess.

You have no vision on that side. Even after all these years, turning your head to compensate remains an unconscious habit.

And yet...

You smile.

Such a beautiful memory.

Veyr Pass. Winter. Artillery lighting the snow orange. Your battalion wavering as the enemy broke through the eastern gate. A shard of steel tearing across your face. The sudden darkness on one side, the hot blood, the order to retreat.

And your own voice, perfectly steady: “Anyone who runs answers to me.”

You held the pass.

You lost an eye. The Directorate gained a victory. A fair exchange, you have always thought.

Your smile deepens. The scars are not flaws to conceal; they are proof that you endured what lesser officers could not. Even the phantom ache feels almost affectionate now, like an old comrade reminding you of a splendid, terrible day.

You wouldn't trade them for the prettiest face in the Directorate.

After all, beauty fades.

Victory is eternal.

Your remaining eye wanders toward the medals decorating your chest.

Your gaze drops to the medals.

The crimson ribbon commemorates Veyr Pass. The bronze medal honors the Northern Pacification. And that magnificent silver starburst?

The suppression of the Third Army Mutiny.

You remember every detail.

The frightened conspirators. Their pathetic excuses. The wonderful moment when their arrogance dissolved into helpless terror.

A soft chuckle escapes your lips.

How you adored watching defiance disappear from their faces.

There is something almost indecent about the satisfaction it gives you. That delicious tightening in your stomach when a particularly arrogant opponent realizes you hold absolute power over their fate.

You especially enjoy the stubborn ones.

Their eventual obedience is so much sweeter.

Even your own soldiers know better than to disappoint you. You've turned spoiled aristocrats into trembling cadets, reduced insubordinate veterans to exhausted silence, and transformed entire companies through your merciless discipline.

You enjoy the process rather more than a respectable officer ought to admit.

But you are a respectable officer.

And results speak for themselves.

A strange thought briefly interrupts your satisfaction. Somewhere, there was a man who stared at women like you with a peculiar mixture of longing and dread. He found your ruthless confidence beautiful. He imagined your boots, your medals, your cruel little smile.

How embarrassing.

You cannot quite remember his face. Perhaps he lives here. That would explain the mess, the cheap toiletries, the childish decorations.

Your nose wrinkles.

“Pathetic,” you say.

Your voice is a low, elegant contralto, clipped by years of issuing orders. It surprises you for half a heartbeat, then feels so natural that the surprise itself seems absurd. You roll a word across your tongue—“Discipline”—and savor how effortlessly it becomes a threat.

The thought of this room’s owner makes you bite your lower lip. What a delicious project he would be: straighten his posture, confiscate his distractions, strip away every excuse, make him stand before you until even his breathing awaited permission.

You could make something useful of him.

Or perhaps you would simply enjoy watching him try.

You adjust your cap, rest a gloved hand on your sabre, and meet your own gaze in the mirror. The cruel smile returns.

There you are, Colonel.

Perfectly at home in your skin.

And thoroughly out of place in this dreadful bathroom.
